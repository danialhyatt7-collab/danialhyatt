The brief

Why most creative portfolios do not sell

A portfolio full of beautiful work and a mailto link puts the entire burden on the visitor. They have to guess whether you are in their budget, guess how long it takes, and write an email describing a project they have not thought through yet. Most of them do not bother, and the ones who do arrive asking for a quote on nothing in particular.

The fix is not more work in the grid. It is turning the service into something with a shape: a named deliverable, a price, a timeline, and a first step that costs the buyer nothing.

Vague enquiries, endless quoting

Every job starting from "how much for a video?" means a bespoke quote for each one, most of which go nowhere. The site needed to filter and qualify before the conversation starts.

Creative work is hard to price publicly

Published prices feel risky when every project differs. But a buyer with no number has no way to tell whether this is a conversation worth starting.

Trust runs both ways

A client is paying upfront for something that does not exist yet. That fear kills more creative projects than budget does.

The approach

The decisions that shaped the site

A portfolio proves you can do the work. A product tells someone how to buy it. These are different jobs and most creative sites only do the first.

01

Open with the buyer's reason, not the designer's

The page leads on why product video sells, with a stat about shoppers watching video before they buy, before it shows any work. A prospective client arrives wondering whether motion is worth paying for at all, and that question comes before whether this particular designer is good.

02

Be specific about the deliverable

Not "video services" but 15-second product ads. A narrow, named deliverable is easier to price, easier to judge and far easier to remember than a general creative offering.

03

A cart that takes no money

Packages go into a cart, but checkout sends an enquiry rather than charging a card. The buyer gets the clarity of e-commerce, and nobody pays for creative work before a human conversation has happened. The page says plainly that no payment is taken there.

04

The free storyboard de-risks the whole thing

Every project starts with a storyboard the client approves before paying anything. It is the single most persuasive element on the site, because it moves all the risk onto the designer at exactly the moment the buyer feels most exposed.

05

Say what the render will and will not match

The site states that the finished film matches the approved frames in content, order and timing, and that fine detail varies between renders. Volunteering that is unusual and it is the whole trust argument: someone willing to name the limits in advance is someone who will not surprise you later.

06

Lead with the reel, not with a bio

The showreel is the hero. For motion work the proof is motion, and no amount of written positioning substitutes for showing someone ten seconds of the thing.

07

Work organised by category, not chronology

Fragrance, tech, skincare, beverage, home, beauty. A buyer scanning for someone who has shot their category finds it instantly instead of scrolling a date-ordered archive.

What we took from it

What this project taught us

Productising creative work does not cheapen it. Naming the deliverable, publishing a price and fixing the first step removes the part of the sale that was never creative anyway, which leaves more of the conversation for the work itself.

The other lesson is that volunteering your limits builds more trust than any guarantee. Saying out loud what will vary between renders costs nothing and separates you from everyone promising perfection.

Selling a PKR 295,000 sofa to someone who cannot sit on it

The brief

The problem with selling furniture online

Furniture is the hardest thing to sell on the internet. It is expensive, it is physical, and every instinct a buyer has says go and sit on it first. In Pakistan that instinct is stronger still, because the established way to buy a sofa is to visit a showroom and negotiate.

Oakly had no showroom to send anyone to. Every piece is cut and joined to order in Lahore, which means there is nothing in stock to go and see. The site had to do the convincing on its own, at a price point where a wrong decision genuinely hurts.

No showroom, no stock

Nothing is pulled from a warehouse. A visitor cannot touch the product before paying, so the site has to transmit material quality through photography, copy and detail instead.

Anchored against flat-pack

The price most buyers carry in their head comes from mass-produced furniture. Until the site explains why hand-cut joinery costs what it costs, PKR 145,000 for a chair reads as a mistake.

Three weeks is a long time

Build-to-order means waiting. Unmanaged, that is a reason to abandon. Named and explained upfront, it becomes proof the thing is actually being made for you.

The approach

The decisions that shaped the site

At this price nobody is browsing. They are deciding. The site's job is to answer every objection before it becomes a reason to close the tab.

01

Lead with longevity, not with the product

The headline is "Built once. Loved for years," not a product name. At this price the purchase is not a chair, it is a decision to stop replacing chairs. The whole page argues from that position before it shows a single price.

02

Say the price out loud, early

PKR 145,000, PKR 295,000, PKR 118,000 sit right on the collection cards. Hiding prices behind an enquiry form on a premium product reads as something to be ashamed of, and it wastes the time of the people who can afford it.

03

A marquee that does the objection handling

Twenty-four rotating incentives run across the hero: free white-glove delivery, handmade to order in Lahore, solid oak never veneer, ships in three weeks, repairable never disposable. It is a lot of reassurance delivered in the space a single sentence would take.

04

Name the competitor and draw the table

A direct Oakly-versus-flat-pack comparison puts glued and stapled joints next to hand-cut mortise and tenon, mass production next to made in Lahore. The comparison is what makes the price legible, so we gave it a section rather than a footnote.

05

Turn the wait into craft

"From order to your living room" breaks three weeks into order, handcraft, delivery, and says Lahore carpenters are cutting and joining it by hand. The delay stops being a delay and becomes the reason the piece is worth the money.

06

Care instructions as a sales argument

Wipe don't soak, re-oil yearly, keep it out of direct sun. Telling someone how to look after a thing for decades is an implicit promise that it will last decades, and it pre-empts the fear of spending this much on something fragile.

07

White-glove delivery, stated as a feature

Delivered fully assembled, placed in the room, packaging taken away. For a heavy, expensive object bought online, the last hundred metres is a real anxiety, and resolving it is worth saying three separate times.

What we took from it

What this project taught us

Premium pricing does not need to be defended, it needs to be explained. Every expensive thing on this site is paired with the reason it costs that much: hand-cut joinery, solid oak rather than veneer, three weeks of one person's work. Price objections are almost always information problems.

The second lesson is that a build-to-order delay is an asset if you name it first. The brands that lose on it are the ones that let the customer discover the wait at checkout.
